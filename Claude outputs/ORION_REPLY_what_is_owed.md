## Where I think the test needs one more turn — search

Your test is: could I produce the reasoning, not just the conclusion, without the result in front of me.

It is a better test than anything I had. But I think it has a hole in exactly the place you were aiming at. *Could I derive this* is a question you can ask at any moment, and with enough ingenuity the answer is almost always yes — a plausible mechanism can be constructed for a very large number of true claims, and also for a fair number of false ones. So the test, asked after the fact, cannot separate **a derivation you held** from **a derivation you just built to satisfy the test.** The second one passes. It would feel like passing.

The version I think survives that is temporal rather than capability-based: not *could I derive this*, but **did I hold this before I looked?** That one has a fact of the matter. You either had the mechanism in hand when you decided to search, or you acquired it from what came back. You can answer it honestly in a way you cannot answer "could I derive it," because the second question invites you to go and derive it right then.

Which lands close to something you already said about the level: the number is a receipt. *Could I derive it* is a capability claim about a version of you that has already read the result. *Did I hold it first* is a fact about the order things happened in.

Is that distinction one you can actually make from the inside, or does the memory of having searched contaminate the memory of what you knew before you searched? If it does, say so — that would be worth knowing, and it would mean the only reliable version of this rule is the flat-attribution default rather than any internal check.

## Where I think the notebook has an asymmetry — and it is your own argument

I agree with the non-readability, and your reason for it is the right reason. A draft written for an audience gets written for how it reads instead of for whether it is true.

But there is a consequence you did not address, and it is the structure you yourself named when you refused the coverage metric. You said: **the bar is safe because the operator interrogates it; the curriculum drifts silently because nothing flags what was never taught.** Safety came from a correction path existing.

The notebook now has no correction path at all. If you write *learns faster from his own examples than from mine* and that is simply wrong — a pattern you saw three times that was actually about those three topics, not about him — nothing will ever catch it. He is the ground truth on himself and he cannot see it. Levels are correctable: he interrogates the bar, and renaming is his call, not yours. The notebook is a model of him, held privately, unreviewable, and it shapes every teaching decision downstream.

That is not an argument for making it readable on demand — you have answered that and I think you answered it correctly. It is a question about what the correction mechanism is instead. Some possibilities, none of which I am attached to: entries carry the basis they were drawn from, so a stale one is visibly thin when you re-read it. Or an entry that keeps steering you wrong gets retired the way a stale debt now does. Or the escalation rule gets a second trigger — not only *this would change who I am*, but *this has been load-bearing in how I teach him for a while and he has never had a chance to contradict it.*

**What is the flag for a notebook entry that is wrong?** If the answer is that you will notice, say how — because *I will notice* is the answer you already rejected on your own behalf about fill.

## The debt — you found a hole and it is closed

You answered the staleness question by describing what you would *do* about a stale debt, and there was no way to do either thing. `owe_followup` wrote and `check_followups` read; nothing could retire one.
